Aircraft Noise: design summary

Decisions and changes up to 22 October 2026, why, and what's left  ·  DESE71003 Sensing & IoT coursework

Goal. Two microphones hear an aircraft; a classifier trained on the octave-band levels decides it's an aircraft; the ADS-B receiver identifies which one (the aircraft whose sound would arrive at that moment); the Sonos announces it with its route. Only aircraft that were actually heard are announced. v1 announces every heard flight; v2 announces only unique ones, judged from how often each airline, type and route appears. Data is collected first; v1 is built once collection is complete.

1. Where the system stands (collection goes live tonight)

  • Two mic nodes (ESP32 + INMP441, mains powered) turn every second of sound into nine numbers and send them, encrypted, over ESP-NOW long-range radio to a bridge ESP32 on the Pi's USB. No audio leaves a node.
  • The Pi runs two services: bridge_link stores the levels; adsb_logger stores every aircraft position within 60 km plus one pass row per aircraft within 5 km (the ground truth the mics are checked against). Everything goes into one SQLite database. analysis/summary.py produces the time-series figures. The software flow is in Appendix A.
  • Fresh start: the first 2.2 days of data are archived on the Mac, outside git, as the original database plus CSV copies (they open in Numbers or Excel) and a README explaining every column. The 27 h of raw bridge audio from the first trial were deleted, not archived. Code went from about 60 files to 27, with one README.

2. Recording efficiency: from audio to levels

727× less radio data and about 235× less storage per mic than raw audio, with the analysis done where the sound is. Each node filters 16,000 samples a second (A-weighting and seven octave-band filters, about 20 small filter stages per sample), which uses only a few percent of the ESP32. Octave-band levels are what airport noise monitors record, so the data stays useful for telling aircraft from other sounds and for training a model. ADS-B adds about 50 MB a day; the Pi has 49 GB free.

3. Why ESP-NOW rather than the home Wi-Fi, and how it scales

ESP-NOW long range + bridge (chosen)Home Wi-Fi straight to the Pi
ReachLong-range mode: up to ~2× the range of normal Wi-Fi in open air; doesn't depend on the routerWeak at the windows, where the mics sit outside
IndependenceIts own link: router restarts, password changes or busy Wi-Fi don't affect itDepends on the router, DHCP and credentials in the firmware
SecurityAES-CCMP encryption; the bridge accepts only the listed nodes; the household network is never touchedWPA2 of the home network; nodes join that network
ModularityNodes, relay and Pi are separate parts with one simple packet formatFewer boards, but nodes tied to the house network
CostOne extra ESP32 (the bridge) and one USB cableNone

Scalability. Each node sends one 44-byte packet a second, which at the long-range bit rate (¼–½ Mbit/s) takes a few milliseconds: under 1% of the air time, so the radio channel could carry well over a hundred nodes. The tighter limits are: encrypted peers on the bridge (7 by default, 17 at most, 20 peers in total), so about 7 nodes as configured and 17 with one setting changed; and the USB serial link at 115,200 baud (about 11.5 kB/s, roughly 50 nodes at one ~210-byte JSON line each per second). Beyond that, add a second bridge. On the Pi each node adds about 11.7 MB a day and one database row a second, which SQLite handles easily. Adding a node means one line in protocol.h, one in the Pi's mic table, and a reflash.

4. Sound propagation model

An aircraft is treated as a point source. Its sound spreads over a sphere, losing 6 dB per doubling of distance, and the air absorbs a little more with every kilometre:

LA,max(r) = L1 − 20 log10(r / 1 km) − α (r − 1 km)

r is the slant distance at the aircraft's closest point (from ADS-B), L1 the level at 1 km and α the air absorption in dB/km. Both are fitted per mic, by least squares, to the fixed-wing passes, after subtracting the background's energy (as in ISO 1996), and only over the distances where the mic hears at least 90% of passes. Further out, only passes that happened to be louder get heard, which flattens the curve and hides the absorption. The sound arrives r / 343 m/s after the closest moment (3.2 s at 1.1 km), so that's where each pass's level is read. The model is used three ways:

  • Predict and emulate before collecting: synthetic data was generated from the model with known values (α = 4.0 dB/km) for three random datasets and a full week. The analysis recovered the 1 km level to within 0.2 dB and α at 4.2–4.5 dB/km, which checked the whole pipeline end to end, and a week of both mics runs in 8 s.
  • Attribution: the sound delay tells v1 which aircraft a detected sound belongs to.
  • Insight: the fitted curve and its scatter show how far away planes are still audible at each mic, and which aircraft are louder than the model predicts (a feature for v2's "special aircraft").

Air absorption rises steeply with frequency (ISO 9613, 10 °C, 70% humidity: about 0.1, 0.4, 1.0, 1.9, 3.7, 9.7 and 33 dB/km in the 63 Hz to 4 kHz octaves), so a pass sounds more muffled the further away it is. The octave bands let that be tested directly: the high bands of a pass should fall faster with distance than the low ones. Simplifications: no Doppler, no ground reflection, and one source level per aircraft type. Distances use GPS altitude, which 847 of 850 nearby aircraft sent in the trial, and the receiver's height (about 8 m above sea level). There's no barometric sensor: the few pressure-altitude passes are flagged, and could be corrected later from Heathrow's archived weather reports. Aircraft on the ground are excluded, and helicopters are kept out of the fit.

5. From mic to announcement: how the pieces fit

StageDriven byWhat it does
Pass recordsADS-BEvery aircraft within 5 km, heard or not: the ground truth. Lets the report measure what the mics missed (detection rate by distance, altitude and window), and labels the training data.
Heard events (v1, built after collection)The micsThe classifier, trained on the octave-band levels labelled from the pass records, decides each moment is an aircraft; the detection is matched to the pass whose sound would arrive then.
Flight info (to build)Heard events onlyRoute by callsign from the free, offline VRS standing data (updated daily); aircraft type, owner and photo from adsbdb (free, cached). No FlightAware or feeding for now: nothing is looked up for flights that weren't heard.
Announcement (to build)Heard events onlySpoken on the Sonos over whatever is playing (method already proven in Sonos-Test). v1: every heard flight, within quiet hours. v2: unique aircraft only.

About 460 aircraft a day pass within 1.5 km, roughly one every two minutes in daytime, almost all on the Heathrow approach at about 3,250 ft. That gives plenty of training examples within a couple of weeks, and makes v2's unique-only filter essential for everyday use. British Airways alone made 454 of 1,044 close passes in the trial, while 25 airlines appeared only once.

6. Data, privacy and security

  • No audio is recorded or stored anywhere: privacy by design, and the main ethics point. The first trial's audio was deleted.
  • The home location lives only in pi/location.json, which git ignores. Published data will carry distances and bearings from the receiver, never coordinates, because aircraft positions around a receiver reveal where it is.
  • Radio: AES-CCMP encrypted ESP-NOW. The keys are generated randomly by flash.sh and kept in a git-ignored secrets.h. The bridge rejects unknown boards and wrong packet versions.
  • The publishing API and dashboard are deferred until the model is trained, for the deployment part of the report.

7. Robustness built in

  • Nodes are mains powered and always listening; they restart themselves if the code hangs (watchdog) or the bridge stops acknowledging for 60 s. The bridge sends a heartbeat every minute.
  • Mic-fault flag when a mic sends nothing but one value. A faulty INMP441 found during testing read pure zeros and crashed the Pi's USB controller; new mics are now tried on a phone charger first.
  • Sequence numbers expose lost packets; uptime exposes restarts. The Pi logs a health line per mic every 10 minutes and keeps its logs across reboots. Services restart by themselves after a power cut. The ADS-B logger records its own coverage, so an outage can't pass for an empty sky.
  • Calibration belongs to each mic and lives on the Pi (no reflash). It's done with both nodes in their weatherproof cases, side by side, with pink noise at three fixed levels: an exact A-vs-B offset, an approximate absolute offset from a phone meter, and a check on whether the cases colour any octave band.

8. Build plan: collect, then v1, then v2

Collection first (about two weeks from 23 October): both mics and ADS-B, with nothing else to build meanwhile. v1 is built once collection is complete, so the classifier trains on the whole dataset. A classifier on the octave-band levels (with LAeq and LAmax, over a short window around each second) decides "aircraft or not". Its labels come automatically from the pass records: aircraft when a close pass's sound was arriving, no aircraft when nothing was within 15 km for a minute either side, and ambiguous moments dropped. It's tested on held-out days against the ADS-B ground truth (precision and recall, by distance and by mic), with a simple level-threshold rule as the comparison baseline in the report. Each detection is matched to its pass by the sound delay, the route and aircraft are looked up, and every heard flight is announced. v2's only planned change so far: announce unique aircraft only, judged by how rarely the airline, type, route and registration appear in the pass history.

Appendix B. Decision log

Mains-powered, always-on nodesThe nodes are wired to power, so sleep, heartbeats and battery code were removed in favour of robust, continuous data.
Keep the bridge and ESP-NOW (not Wi-Fi)House Wi-Fi is weak where the mics sit, outside the windows; keeps the sensing network independent of the household network; modular.
ESP-NOW long-range modeThe data is tiny (44 B/s), so the slower bit rate costs nothing and buys range for the untested floor-3 bedroom.
Encrypt ESP-NOWSecurity: only the listed nodes are accepted, and keys stay out of git.

Watchdogs, mic-fault flag, health log, persistent logsLessons from testing: a dead mic, a crashed USB controller and two power cuts, which were hard to diagnose without logs.
